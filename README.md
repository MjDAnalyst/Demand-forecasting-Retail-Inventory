# Demand-forecasting-Retail-Inventory
Demand Forecasting for Retail Inventory

Stackron Academy Mentorship Program — Retail & E-Commerce Track

The Problem

A mid sized retail chain is dealing with a classic inventory headache: some products keep running out (lost sales), while others just sit on shelves tying up cash. Leadership wants to move from reactive restocking to something more data driven,better forecasts, smarter reorder timing, and promotions that are actually timed well instead of guessed at.

This project works through that problem end to end: cleaning the data, digging into what's actually driving (or not driving) demand, building forecasting models, and putting together a dashboard that surfaces stockout/overstock risk at a glance.

Dataset

[Retail Store Inventory Forecasting Dataset (Kaggle)](https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/anirudhchauhan/retail-store-inventory-forecasting-dataset) — 73,100 daily records across 5 stores, 20 products, 5 categories, and 4 regions, from Jan 2022 to Jan 2024. Includes sales, inventory levels, pricing, discounts, holiday/promotion flags, and weather.

Worth flagging upfront: this is a synthetic dataset, and it behaves like one. A big part of this project's value ended up being figuring that out and being honest about it, rather than forcing a narrative onto data that doesn't have one.

Approach

1. Data cleaning & EDA — checked for missing values, duplicates, and outliers. The dataset itself was clean (no nulls, no duplicates), but flagged ~673 rows with a negative `Demand Forecast` value and ~715 statistical outliers in `Units Sold` (kept in, not dropped — they look like genuine high-demand days).
2.Volatility analysis — compared coefficient of variation across products, stores, and regions.
3. Seasonality analysis — checked day-of-week, monthly, and labeled-season patterns.
4.Promotion impact analysis — compared units sold on promotion/discount days vs. not, including a discount-tier breakdown.
5. Forecasting models — chronological train/test split (last ~20% of the date range held out), a 7-day rolling mean baseline, and a Random Forest model with lag features.
6. Dashboard — built an interactive Power BI version, covering forecast vs. actual, stockout/overstock risk by region/category, and seasonality/promotion impact.

Key Findings

- Volatility, seasonality, and promotions all came back flat.
- Coefficient of variation sits at ~0.78–0.81 across every single product, store, and region — no segment is meaningfully more unpredictable than another.
- Day of the week, monthly, and season averages are all within ~2% of each other. No real seasonal curve.
- Average units sold on promotion days (136.4) vs. non-promotion days (136.5) — essentially identical. Same story across every discount tier (0% through 20%), all landing within a couple percentage points of each other.

I want to be upfront about this rather than dress it up: this isn't a case of the analysis missing something. It's the dataset itself being synthetic, it doesn't encode the kind of real seasonal/promotional demand drivers you'd expect from actual retail transactions. Reporting "no significant pattern found" honestly is a legitimate conclusion, and I think it's more useful to a reviewer than manufacturing a story that isn't backed by the numbers.

Forecasting models topped out at the data's noise ceiling.

| Model | MAE | RMSE | MAPE |
|---|---|---|---|
| Naive (overall mean) | 88.75 | 108.17 | 344.6% |
| Baseline: 7-day rolling mean | 93.19 | 115.45 | 347.9% |
| Random Forest (lag features + categoricals) | 88.90 | 108.24 | 346.2% |

Neither model meaningfully beats a naive average — because there's no learnable signal in the features to exploit. This is a direct consequence of the flat volatility/seasonality/promotion findings above: if nothing in the data actually moves demand, no amount of model complexity can forecast it better.

One more thing worth flagging: the dataset's own `Demand Forecast` column correlates 0.997 with actual `Units Sold` and differs from it by a small, tight offset (mean +5, range -10 to +20). That's a strong sign it was generated by taking the actual sales and adding synthetic noise, not an independent forecast. It significantly outperforms both models above, but it's not a fair benchmark, since it leaks the target it's supposed to be predicting.

Overstock, not stockout, is the real structural risk.
- Stockout risk (inventory ≤ that day's sales) is rare: ~0.4–0.6% of records, flat across every region and category.
- Overstock risk (inventory > 3× that day's sales) is common: ~33–34% of records, again uniform across regions and categories.

If this were a real retail operation, this is the headline finding: the business is much more likely to be sitting on excess inventory than running out of stock.

Recommendations

- Prioritize inventory reduction strategies (tighter reorder points, markdown timing) over stockout prevention buffers, given how one sided the risk actually is.
- Treat the existing `Demand Forecast` field with caution if it's used elsewhere in the business, its near-perfect correlation with actual sales suggests it may not be a genuine forward-looking forecast.
- For a production version of this project, prioritize getting real transactional data even a few months of it, over refining models further on synthetic data with no real demand drivers.

Limitations
- Synthetic data. As covered above, the dataset doesn't appear to encode realistic demand drivers — volatility, seasonality, and promotion effects are all statistically flat. Findings should be read as a demonstration of methodology, not as real retail insight.
- Leaky benchmark column. The dataset's `Demand Forecast` field appears derived from actual sales, so it isn't used as a genuine model benchmark.
- No external demand drivers. Real world factors like local events, competitor stockouts, or economic conditions aren't present in this dataset and would likely add real signal a synthetic dataset can't provide.
